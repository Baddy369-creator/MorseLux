How to decode Morse code from sound

  1. Start the microphone and allow access when your browser asks. You have 30 free seconds per day.
  2. Hold your device close to the loudspeaker, radio or the person sending — about 10–30 cm. Keep background noise low.
  3. Watch the signal bar. Every beep shows up as a line, and the text appears live. The decoder finds the tone (300–3500 Hz) and the speed automatically.
  4. Or open a file — a recording, a voice memo or a video with Morse code. Files are unlimited.

Tips for better results

  • Go close. The closer you are to the loudspeaker, the less echo from the room reaches the microphone.
  • One signal at a time. Music, speech or two Morse signals at once confuse every decoder.
  • Give it a few letters. The decoder learns the speed from the first characters and gets more accurate after that.
  • Practice your ear. A decoder is a great help, but nothing beats understanding Morse yourself — try the free Morse code trainer (also a good CW trainer for radio amateurs).

Frequently asked questions

How does the Morse code decoder work?

It listens for the tone that switches on and off, measures how long each beep and each pause lasts and learns the speed from the difference between short (dots) and long (dashes) beeps. Pauses tell it where a letter or a word ends. The first letters can be corrected once it has learned the speed.

Which sounds can it decode?

Morse code sent as a clear tone between about 300 and 3500 Hz: recordings, YouTube videos, radio (CW), apps, buzzers and whistles. It works best with one signal at a time and little background noise. Strong echo in a small, bare room, several signals at once or loud speech make it harder.

Which speeds work?

From very slow (about 3 words per minute) up to about 50 WPM, also with Farnsworth timing (extra-long pauses). Very irregular hand-sent Morse can produce wrong letters.

Is the microphone free?

Yes, for 30 seconds per day. After that the microphone pauses until the next day. Decoding audio files and the example stay unlimited. For unlimited live decoding there is the Morse Lux app for iPhone (live decoding is part of Morse Lux Pro).

Can I decode an audio file?

Yes. Press “Open audio file” and choose an MP3, WAV, M4A, AAC or FLAC file — or a video. OGG works in most browsers but not in every Safari version. A one-minute recording takes about a second on a computer; long recordings take longer, especially on a phone.

Is my audio uploaded or recorded?

No. Everything happens on your device. The sound from the microphone is analyzed live and immediately discarded; files are never uploaded.

Why does it show # or wrong letters?

A # means a pattern that is not a Morse character. Common reasons: background noise, echo, two signals at once or very uneven timing. Move closer to the loudspeaker, turn the volume up a little and make the room quieter.

Can it read light signals (flashlight)?

Not on the website – this tool works with sound only. Decoding light signals with the camera is in the Morse Lux iPhone app as part of Morse Lux Pro.
