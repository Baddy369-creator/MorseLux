About Morse Lux

Morse Lux is an iPhone app and this free website for learning, writing and reading Morse code. No ads, no account, no tracking.

Updated 7 November 2026

Who we are

Morse Lux comes from Spielkinder, a small independent app studio. We build the iPhone app and this website ourselves – every tool here is something we wanted to use, and we test it before it goes online.

Why this site exists

Most Morse code websites are full of ads and pop-ups. Morse Lux wants to be the opposite: fast, clear and free – a translator, an alphabet you can print, a trainer that teaches by ear, a decoder that listens, and the Morse keyboard for the iPhone.

How we test the decoder

Before every change, the audio decoder has to pass a test bench of computer-generated Morse signals with known content. Results of the current version (share of correctly decoded characters):

Test signalResult
Clean signal, 3 to 55 words per minute98–100 %
Hand keying with 30 % timing jitter100 %
Farnsworth spacing (e.g. 18/5, 20/10)99–100 %
Pitch 300 to 3500 Hz, whistled Morse100 %
Noise, signal 10 dB above the noise100 %
Noise, 6 dB (audio file)about 96 %
Noise, 3 dB (audio file)about 79 %
Fading signal (QSB, 1 Hz)about 97 %

Known limits: strong room echo, very weak signals and two Morse signals at the same loudness. We say so openly in the decoder's FAQ.

Privacy first

Everything runs in your browser. Sound from the microphone and audio files are never uploaded. Details are in the privacy policy.

Frequently asked questions

Who is behind Morse Lux?

Morse Lux is made by Spielkinder, an independent app studio. Contact: MorseLuxsupport@protonmail.com.

Why is the website free and ad-free?

The website is meant to be a clean, useful place to start with Morse code. It is paid for by the optional Pro version of the iPhone app.

Which Morse code standard do you use?

The International Morse Code as defined by the ITU (Recommendation ITU-R M.1677), plus common extra letters such as Ä, Ö, Ü, Ñ and the usual procedural signs.
