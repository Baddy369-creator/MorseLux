How to Learn Morse Code

The fastest way to learn Morse code is by ear: start with two letters at full speed, add one at a time and practise a few minutes every day. Here is the method, step by step.

Why learning by sound works best

Morse code is a language of rhythm. Experienced operators don't hear “dot dash” — they hear “di-dah” and know it is an A, the same way you recognise a word without spelling it. If you learn from a chart and count signals, you build an extra step into your head that later caps your speed. So the rule is simple: learn every sign as a sound.

Step by step

  1. Start with two characters. Our Morse trainer starts with K and M, the app with E and T – both work. Listen until you tell the two apart without thinking.
  2. Add one letter at a time. When you get about 9 out of 10 right, add the next one. Mixing new letters with known ones keeps them fresh.
  3. Keep the speed up. Letters at around 18–20 words per minute stop you from counting. If it is too fast, make the pauses longer, not the letters.
  4. Write what you hear. Copying letters on paper or a keyboard trains the link from sound to letter.
  5. Send it too. Tap the rhythm on your phone or a key. Sending and receiving reinforce each other.
  6. Move to words and sentences. Once you know about 15 letters, short words like TEN, SEA or NOTE make practice real.
  7. Practise daily. 10–15 minutes every day beats one hour on the weekend. Stop while it is still fun.

The rhythm of Morse code

All timing is based on one unit, the length of a dot. A dash is three units. Inside a letter there is one unit of silence, between letters three and between words seven. Speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), based on the word PARIS, which is exactly 50 units long.

Common mistakes

  • Learning from a chart. Good for looking things up, bad for learning. Use the Morse alphabet with sound.
  • Practising too slowly. Slow letters can be counted — and counting becomes a habit that is hard to break.
  • Long, rare sessions. Your brain learns rhythms through repetition over many days.
  • Only receiving or only sending. Do both.

Learning order that works

There is no single correct order, but many courses start with letters that are short and sound clearly different. A popular Koch-method sequence is: K M R S U A P T L O W I . N J E F 0 Y , V G 5 / Q 9 Z H 3 8 B ? 4 2 7 C 1 D 6 X. The Morse trainer on this site uses exactly this order; the MorseLux app starts with E and T. In the end the order does not matter much – you learn the same 40 sounds.

Your 10-minute plan for every day

  1. 2 minutes warm-up. Listen once to the characters from yesterday.
  2. 5 minutes trainer. One or two rounds in the Morse trainer; at 90 % a new character joins.
  3. 2 minutes sending. Tap a short word, or type it in the translator and listen.
  4. 1 minute fun. Solve the Morse puzzle of the day.

Ten minutes a day beat one hour at the weekend: the ear learns in small portions.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to learn Morse code?

It depends on how often you practise. With 10–20 minutes every day, many people recognise all letters by ear within a few weeks. Copying whole conversations at higher speeds takes months of regular practice.

What is the Koch method?

A training method developed by the German psychologist Ludwig Koch. You start with just two characters played at your target speed, and add a new character only when you copy about 90% correctly. Because the speed never changes, you learn to recognise sounds instead of counting dots and dashes.

Should I learn Morse code with pictures or mnemonics?

They help at the very beginning but slow you down later, because you have to translate the picture back into a sound. Learning each letter as a sound pattern from the start is faster in the long run.

What speed should I start with?

Many teachers recommend letters at about 18–20 WPM with longer pauses between them (Farnsworth timing), so each letter keeps its natural rhythm. Slower letters tempt you to count.

Do I need Morse code for a ham radio licence?

No longer in most countries, including the USA and Germany. Many radio amateurs still learn it because CW (Morse) gets through when voice does not, and because it is fun.
